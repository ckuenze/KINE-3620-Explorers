Sprinting Kinetics Explorer

Three windows: how force, velocity, power, and momentum relate across a simulated sprint; how the net impulse of one ground contact changes momentum; and how leg stiffness changes a single ground contact. Horizontal is +y (anterior, the running direction).

Pre-class: work alone through the guided steps, then answer the check questions for the reading's "Before class" list.

In-class: partner activity. Predict first, test it with the tool, then talk through the discussion questions.

Pre-class: guided steps

  1. Window 1: drag Time in race from 0 s to the finish. Watch force fall while velocity rises. Find when power peaks.
  2. Window 2: keep the defaults and check the arithmetic: J = 500 N × 0.150 s, then Δv = J ÷ m.
  3. Window 2: drag the net force below zero (braking wins). What happens to Δv?
  4. Window 3: press Lower stiffness (12 kN/m), then Higher stiffness (28 kN/m) (or drag the leg stiffness slider). Watch contact time, peak force, ΔL and Δy.

Check yourself

Give the equations and units for momentum, power, and impulse.

Momentum p = m·v (kg·m/s). Power P = F·v (W, or N·m/s). Impulse J = F·Δt (N·s). Note that 1 N·s = 1 kg·m/s.

State the impulse–momentum theorem in your own words.

The net impulse on the body equals its change in momentum: F_net·Δt = m·Δv. Only the net force (propulsive minus braking) changes speed.

What does leg stiffness mean in the loading and unloading phases of stance?

k_leg = F_peak ÷ ΔL. In loading, the leg compresses (ΔL grows) as force rises. In unloading, it lengthens again as force falls. A stiffer leg compresses less for the same force.

How does vertical stiffness differ from leg stiffness?

k_vert = F_peak ÷ Δy uses the drop of the center of mass (Δy), not the leg's own compression (ΔL). Because the leg sweeps through an angle, Δy is smaller than ΔL, so k_vert is larger than k_leg. Studies do not clearly rank which one matters more for sprint performance.

In-class: partner challenge

  1. Predict: does peak power come at the start, the middle, or top speed? Then test: scrub Window 1 and read the power curve.
  2. Predict: an 80 kg sprinter, −200 N net force, 100 ms contact, entering at 10.00 m/s. What is the new speed? Then test: set it in Window 2.
  3. Predict: switch to the 200 m. Where does it lose the most speed? Then test: compare the velocity curves.
  4. Predict: which goes up more from lower to higher stiffness, k_leg or k_vert? Then test: use the presets in Window 3.

Discuss

  • At top speed the sprinter still pushes hard on every step, yet the force curve here is small. Why? (Hint: this is net horizontal force.)
  • Speed = step length × step frequency. How could a change in stiffness or contact time affect each one?
  • Is more stiffness always better? What could go wrong?
