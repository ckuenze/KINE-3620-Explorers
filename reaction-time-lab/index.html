Reaction Time Lab

Pre-class: measure your simple and choice reaction time on your own, see Hick’s law in your own numbers, and answer the check questions.

How fast are your reflexes, Hoos? Run three short experiments, earn your stat line, and see how your reactions connect to agility.

Pre-class: work alone. Run the light block and the 1, 2 and 4 box blocks, then answer the check questions on the Results tab. No headphones needed.

In-class: the full lab. Predict first, run all three experiments, then save your results code for the class scoreboard.

Pre-class: guided steps (about 8 minutes)

  1. Open 1 Simple. Make your prediction, then run the light block (1 practice trial, then 8 real trials).
  2. Open 2 Choices. Make your prediction, then run the 1 box, 2 box and 4 box blocks.
  3. Open Results. Write down your light median, your 1 box and 4 box medians, and your cost per doubling (b).
  4. Answer the check questions at the bottom of the Results tab. Bring your answers and one question to class.

What you will do (about 15 minutes)

Experiment 1, Simple. Respond to one signal. Is a sound or a light faster?

Experiment 2, Choices. Respond to 1, 2, 3 or 4 possible signals. What does a decision cost?

Experiment 3, Twists. Add rules, fakes and hints. Which one slows you down most?

Before each experiment you make a prediction. Your results page compares your predictions with what happened and shows what your reaction time means on the field or court. At the end, save your results for the class scoreboard.

Before you begin

Sit comfortably with the page in front of you. Silence notifications. Use the keyboard if you can, because it is the most consistent: the Space bar for one signal and the number keys 1 to 4 for the boxes. A tap on a touchscreen also works. Press Esc to stop a block.

For the sound block, use the device speakers or wired headphones. Bluetooth headphones add a delay and make your sound times look slower than they are. No headphones in a noisy room? You can skip the sound block in Experiment 1.

Set your volume so the beep is clear but not loud.

This is a visual and sound timing task. It cannot be done with a screen reader alone. If you use one, do the sound block in In-class mode with the Space bar, and ask your instructor about other options.

Words you will see

StimulusThe signal you respond to, such as a light or a sound.
Reaction time (RT)The time from the stimulus to your response, in milliseconds (ms). 1000 ms is one second.
MedianThe middle value of your trials. One odd trial does not throw it off.
ChoiceA task where you must pick the right response from several options.
Early press (false start)Pressing before the signal, or within 100 ms of it, so it must be a guess. The trial repeats. (In the lecture, anticipation means something else: reading cues early, such as an opponent’s hips.)
AccuracyHow often you gave the right response. Wrong boxes, misses and early presses all lower it.
Hick’s lawChoice RT = a + b·log2 n, where n is the number of choices. RT goes up by about the same amount (b) each time the number of choices doubles.
A note about the numbers. Screens, speakers and keyboards add a small delay, so your times will be longer than in a research lab. Compare your own conditions with each other on the same device. Do not compare your numbers with a classmate who used a different device.

How scoring works

Events that count

Why accuracy counts. A fast guess is not a fast reaction. Each event ranks the adjusted time (inverse efficiency): median RT ÷ proportion correct. Wrong boxes, misses and early presses all lower the proportion correct.

Example. A guesser with a 300 ms median and 75% correct has an adjusted time of 300 ÷ 0.75 = 400 ms. A careful athlete with 340 ms and 100% correct has 340 ms and wins the event. Below the accuracy floor (default 80%), an athlete gets no points in that event.

Each event is won by the lowest adjusted time. Tied scores split the points for those places. Total = points from every counted event, minus the optional extra penalty. Ties on total go to the lower average adjusted time.

Sound is off by default. Bluetooth headphones add delay, and some students skip the sound block. Turn it on only if everyone used speakers or wired headphones.

Older codes. RTLAB1 codes have no accuracy. They are scored on raw time and flagged “no accuracy” in the roster. Ask those students to re-save from the updated lab.

Fair racing. Keyboards, touchscreens and mice add different delays. If the class used mixed devices, use the division buttons (Keys, Touch, Mouse) to crown a winner in each division. Announce these rules before the run.
