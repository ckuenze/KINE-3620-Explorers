Countermovement Jump Explorer

Explore how the force from the ground and the motion of your center of mass (COM) change through one jump, or measure the height of your own jump from a video.

Words you will see

COM (center of mass)The single point where you can treat all of your body mass as if it were concentrated. It is the average position of your body mass.
Force plateA sensor you stand on. It measures how hard you push into it, and how hard it pushes back on you.
vGRF (vertical ground reaction force)The upward push of the ground on you, measured by the force plate. Its unit is the newton (N).
BW (body weight)Your mass × 9.81 m/s². When you stand still the ground pushes up with exactly 1 BW.
AccelerationHow quickly velocity is changing. Up (+Z) is positive and down (−Z) is negative.
+Z and −Z (up and down)Up is positive (+Z) and down is negative (−Z) on every chart. A positive value points up and a negative value points down.
PhaseOne stage of the jump. The colored bands on every chart show where each phase starts and ends.

The example data are a simulated, representative jump for a 75 kg athlete. They are not real athlete data.

COM position (cm). How high your center of mass is compared with standing. Above zero (+Z) means higher than standing. Below zero (−Z) means lower.
COM velocity (m/s). How fast your COM is moving. Up (+Z) is positive and down (−Z) is negative. Zero means it has stopped for an instant.
COM acceleration (m/s²). How quickly velocity is changing. Positive (+Z) means upward and negative (−Z) means downward. In the air it is −9.81 m/s², the pull of gravity.
vGRF. How hard the ground pushes up on you. The dashed line is body weight. Above it, the ground pushes up harder than your weight. Below it, less.

Each point is one moment from the start of the countermovement to takeoff. The curve starts at the standing point (0 cm, 1 BW). It moves left as your COM goes down, then right as your COM comes back up. The area between the curve and the body weight line is the net work. The jump summary table lists net work for each phase.

Two ways to count work

Work is force × distance, but which force? The two columns use the same jump and the same COM motion. They give different answers because they use different forces.

Net work on the COM uses the net force (vGRF − body weight). It tells you how the COM’s motion energy changes. It is positive when the COM speeds up and negative when it slows down. In unweighting the net force and the motion are both downward, so net work is positive.

Work by the ground force alone uses only vGRF. The ground pushes up, so this work is negative whenever the COM moves down, including unweighting, and positive when the COM moves up.

Gravity accounts for the difference: net work = work by the ground force + work by gravity. The signs differ in unweighting, so always check which force a graph or equation is using.

Check your thinking

Try each one using the charts first, then open the answer.

1. At the lowest point, what are COM velocity and COM acceleration?
Move the cursor to the moment velocity crosses zero.Velocity is zero, because the COM has just stopped moving down and is about to move up. Acceleration is large and positive (upward), and vGRF is far above body weight.
2. When vGRF equals body weight at the end of unweighting, what is special about velocity?
Find where the force curve crosses the body weight line.Acceleration is zero there, so velocity has reached its most negative value. The COM is moving down as fast as it ever does.
3. During flight vGRF is zero. What is COM acceleration, and does it depend on jump height?
Read the acceleration trace during flight.Acceleration is −9.81 m/s², the same for every jump. Gravity is the only force, so the jump is a projectile.
4. Which phase gives the COM its takeoff velocity, and how does the impulse–momentum theorem show it?
Compare net impulse and m × takeoff velocity in the summary table.Net impulse (force beyond body weight × time) from the start of the movement to takeoff equals mass × takeoff velocity, because the COM starts at rest. Unweighting adds negative impulse, braking cancels it, and propulsion supplies the positive impulse that sets takeoff velocity.
5. Why do the takeoff velocity and flight time methods give nearly the same jump height here?
Compare the two heights in the summary table.Both assume your COM is at the same height when you leave the ground and when you land. If you land with your knees bent or tuck your legs in the air, the flight time method gives a wrong answer. A video also limits flight time to the length of one frame.

Up is positive (+Z) and down is negative (−Z) on every chart. COM acceleration = (vGRF − body weight) ÷ mass. Velocity comes from adding up acceleration over time, and position comes from adding up velocity, starting when the countermovement begins. In the air only gravity acts, so the COM follows a smooth arc (projectile motion).

Measure your jump height from a video

This uses the flight time method from class: h = g × t² ÷ 8, where t is the time your feet are off the ground. You find the two moments by stepping through your video one frame at a time. Your video stays on your device.

For a normal video, leave both at the same number. For slow motion, the camera number is bigger than the playback number. For example, 240 recorded and 30 playback is 8 times slower.

Optional: estimate your takeoff velocity from the video

Flight time gives one estimate of your takeoff velocity. You can get a second estimate by tracking your center of mass (COM) through the air. You tell the tool where your COM is in several frames, and it fits a parabola to those points. The two estimates will not match exactly, and comparing them is the point of this activity.

  1. Mark takeoff and landing in the video panel above.
  2. Set the scale. Press Set scale, then click two points on something in the video with a known vertical length. The easiest way is to pause on a frame where the jumper stands upright, click the floor at the feet, then click the top of the head. Type that person’s height in cm and press Apply scale.
  3. Click the COM. Press Start clicking. The video jumps to the first frame after takeoff. Click where you think the COM is, about at the hips near the belly button. The video then moves forward one frame. Keep clicking in the same body spot for at least 4 frames, ideally 8 or more, then press Stop clicking.
  4. Read the comparison below. Use Undo last click if you misclick.

Tips: click the same body spot in every frame. Slow motion video makes this much easier. Your COM position is an estimate, so some difference between the two methods is expected.

How to film a good jump

Follow these tips so the flight time method works well.

  • Film from the side, with your whole body in the frame, and keep the camera still (lean it on something).
  • Jump straight up and land in the same spot on a flat floor.
  • Keep your legs straight in the air. The method assumes you land in the same body position you took off in.
  • Slow motion video (120 or 240 frames per second) gives a more precise answer than normal video.

Words you will see

Takeoff: the first frame where both feet are off the ground.
Landing: the first frame where a foot touches the ground.
Flight time: the time between takeoff and landing.
Frame: one still picture in the video. At 30 frames per second, one frame lasts 0.033 s.
