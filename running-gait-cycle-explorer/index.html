Running Gait Cycle Explorer

Walking vs. running in two views: how the gait cycle is built (double support vs. float phase), and how the vertical ground reaction force (vGRF) curve changes shape, including the impact peak of a rearfoot strike.

Pre-class: work alone through the guided steps, then answer the check questions for the reading's "Before class" list.

In-class: partner activity. Predict first, test it with the tool, then talk through the discussion questions.

Pre-class: guided steps

  1. Look at the Walking bars. Find the two blue double-support blocks (both feet down).
  2. Look at the Running bars at Easy jog. Find the float-phase blocks (neither foot down).
  3. Drag Running pace to Sprint. Watch the stance share shrink and the float phase grow.
  4. In the force chart, compare the walking peaks (just above 1 BW) with the running active peak.
  5. Switch the X-axis to Time (s). Compare where the running curve ends at Easy jog and at Sprint (stance duration).
  6. Press Impact peak and read the rearfoot value in BW. Then press Loading rate: it is the first vGRF peak ÷ the time to that peak.

Check yourself

What one mechanical feature defines running?

A float (flight) phase: a moment in each step when neither foot is on the ground. Speed does not define it.

What happens to double support when walking becomes running?

It disappears. The overlap of two stance phases (both feet down) becomes a gap (no feet down): the float phase.

How do stance time and peak vGRF change from walking to jogging to fast running?

Stance shrinks, as a share of the cycle (about 60% walking, about 40% jogging, 20–25% sprinting) and in seconds. Peak vGRF climbs from just above 1 BW in walking to about 2.0–2.5 BW in jogging and higher with speed. (Step width also narrows with speed; this tool does not model step width.)

In-class: partner challenge

  1. Predict: at what pace does a float phase first appear? Then test: drag the pace slider and watch the Running strip.
  2. Predict: in real time, is a sprint stance longer or shorter than half a walking stance? Then test: switch the x-axis to Time and compare where each curve ends.
  3. Predict: which strike pattern has the steeper early rise? Then test: press Loading rate and read the BW/s values.

Discuss

  • The active (second) peak sits near midstance. Why is that not "push-off"? Where would you look to see propulsion?
  • Two curves can look alike on % of stance and very different in seconds. When does that difference matter?

Vertical ground reaction force: walking vs. running

Each curve is normalized to its own stance (0–100%) so you can compare shapes. Running stance is much shorter in real time; switch the x-axis to Time to see it.

Running curves follow the Running pace slider above: Easy jog. Stance time: walking 0.65 s, running 0.26 s.

Curves are illustrative models of typical shapes (walking peaks just above 1 BW; jogging active peak about 2.0–2.5 BW; faster running higher), not force-plate data from one runner. The rearfoot curve is hidden at Sprint pace because sprinters land on the forefoot. Propulsion and braking show in the anterior–posterior GRF, which this chart does not plot.
