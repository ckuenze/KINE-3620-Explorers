Walking Gait vGRF Explorer

Scrub along one stance phase of walking and see how the vertical ground reaction force (vGRF) curve changes with walking speed. Use the feature buttons to see what each part of the curve means.

Pre-class: work alone through the guided steps, then answer the check questions for the reading's "Before class" list.

In-class: partner activity. Predict first, test it with the tool, then talk through the discussion questions.

Pre-class: guided steps

  1. Stay on Slow. Drag the stance slider from 0% to 100% and watch the vGRF readout.
  2. Press B (first peak) and C (second peak, late stance) to find the two peaks. Both sit just above 1.0 BW at slow speed.
  3. Find where the curve dips below the dashed 1.0 BW line in midstance.
  4. Switch to Fast. Watch the peaks, the dip, and the stance time change.
  5. Drag Body mass. In BW the curve does not change, but the conversion (1.0 BW = m × 9.81 m/s² in N) does. Press N to read the same curve in newtons.

Check yourself

The vGRF is 1.17 BW. Is the COM accelerating up or down?

Up. vGRF = m(g + aCOM), so vGRF above body weight means aCOM > 0 (upward, +z).

How many times does a walking vGRF curve cross 1.0 BW?

Four: rising to the first peak, falling from it into the dip, rising to the second peak, and falling from it before toe off.

Does the stance phase take more or less time as walking speed increases?

Less. The stance time readout drops from Slow to Fast, even though the x-axis stays 0–100% of stance.

In-class: partner challenge

  1. Predict: going from Slow to Fast, does the midstance dip get deeper or shallower? Then test: switch speeds and read the dip.
  2. Predict: the second peak in newtons for a 90 kg walker at Fast (peak BW × 90 kg × 9.81 m/s²). Then test: switch to N, set 90 kg, and press C.
  3. Predict: during right pre-swing, what is the left foot doing? Then test: turn on the both-feet view.
  4. Predict: where in stance does the AP GRF change from braking to propulsive? Then test: turn on the AP panel.

The two curves are the same vGRF shape as above, placed on one gait cycle (right initial contact to the next right initial contact) and offset by half a cycle. Where both feet are loaded, the body is in double support (0–10% and 50–60%). In between, one foot carries the body (single support) while the other limb swings.

Horizontal (anterior–posterior) GRF

Our axes: +y is anterior. Early in stance the foot is ahead of the COM, so the AP GRF points posterior (−y): a braking force. Late in stance the foot is behind the COM, so it points anterior (+y): a propulsive force. In this simple model the switch happens at 50% of stance at every speed.

Discussion prompts

When is vGRF equal to, above, and below body weight?
Equal to BW: at four instants where the curve crosses the dashed 1.0 BW line: rising to and falling from each peak.
Above BW: around the two peaks (B and C). From vGRF = m(g + aCOM), vGRF > W means aCOM > 0: the COM is accelerating upward.
Below BW: in the midstance dip, and also right after contact and just before toe off. vGRF < W means aCOM < 0: the COM is accelerating downward. Near contact and toe off the other foot is also on the ground (double support), so this single-foot curve carries only part of the load.
What's the difference between average and instantaneous loading rate?
Average loading rate is the overall slope of the vGRF curve during load acceptance: here, from initial contact to the B peak. Units are BW/s (or N/s). Many studies instead use a fixed window such as 20–80% of the rise to the first peak [CITE needed], so check the definition before comparing numbers.
Instantaneous loading rate is the steepest slope reached at any moment in that window. It is often the one discussed in injury research.
