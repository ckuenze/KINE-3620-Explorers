What sign does this motion get?

Pick a joint motion and a side. Then point your right thumb along the + end of the axis and check the curl.

Pre-class: guided steps

  1. Pick Shoulder flexion / extension, then Hip abduction / adduction, Trunk or head (C2) rotation and Wrist ulnar / radial deviation. Write down the plane and axis for each.
  2. Pick Elbow flexion / extension (right side), Shoulder abduction / adduction (left side) and Hip internal / external rotation (right side). Which direction is + for each?
  3. Pick Knee flexion / extension and switch between Right side and Left side. Do the same for Knee varus / valgus. Which one flips?
  4. Answer five Quick check questions below. Point your right thumb along the + axis before you answer.

Check yourself

Name the plane and axis for shoulder flexion, hip abduction, trunk rotation and wrist ulnar deviation.

Shoulder flexion: sagittal plane, x (mediolateral) axis. Hip abduction: frontal plane, y (anteroposterior) axis. Trunk rotation: transverse plane, z (longitudinal) axis. Wrist ulnar deviation: frontal plane, y (anteroposterior) axis.

Give the sign of right elbow flexion, left shoulder abduction, right hip external rotation, and turning your head to the right (C2).

Right elbow flexion: +. Left shoulder abduction: +. Right hip external rotation: − (on the right side, internal rotation is +). Turning the head right: − (for midline segments, turning left is +).

Knee flexion gets the same sign on both legs, but knee valgus does not. Why?

Flexion is about the x axis. The thumb points right for both knees, and the curl swings both shanks forward, so extension is + and flexion is − on both sides. Valgus is about the y axis. The curl swings the distal shank toward the left, which is medial (varus) on the right leg and lateral (valgus) on the left leg. So valgus is − on the right and + on the left.

In-class: partner challenge

  1. Predict: stand up and do one movement in each plane. Your partner names the plane, the axis and the sign. Then test: pick that motion here.
  2. Predict: for hip abduction/adduction, hip internal/external rotation, C2 rotation, L5 flexion/extension and wrist ulnar/radial deviation, which direction is + on the right side? Then test: check each one, then switch to the left side.
  3. Predict: hip flexion is +. Is trunk (L5) flexion + too? Then test: pick Trunk (L5) flexion / extension.
  4. Predict: during a landing, a teammate's left knee collapses into valgus. Which plane, which axis, and what sign? Then test: Knee varus / valgus, left side.

Discuss

  • Why do frontal- and transverse-plane signs flip between the right and left sides, while sagittal-plane signs don't?
  • A published figure plots knee flexion as +. What do you check before you read its curves?

Position, velocity, and acceleration: Bolt’s 100 m

The Kinematics Graph Linker, opened on the sprint. Velocity is the slope of position; acceleration is the slope of velocity. Open it in its own window.

Pre-class: guided steps

  1. Press Play. Where is the position curve flattest, and where is it steepest? Steeper means faster.
  2. Turn on Segment averages. Read the five 20 m average velocities on the velocity graph. Which segment is fastest?
  3. Turn on Average (secant). Drag t₁ to 7.92 s and t₂ to 9.58 s (the 80 m and 100 m splits). The line under the result gives the official-split average for that segment. The smooth model leaves out his late slowdown, so its secant is a little higher.
  4. Switch the scenario to Build your own and press Slowing down, still moving forward. Velocity stays + for a while, but acceleration is −.

Check yourself

What was Bolt’s average velocity from 80 m to 100 m? Faster or slower than from 60 m to 80 m?

v = Δx ÷ Δt = 20 m ÷ (9.58 − 7.92) s = 20 ÷ 1.66 = 12.05 m/s. That is slower than 60–80 m (20 ÷ 1.61 = 12.42 m/s). He slowed down over the last 20 m.

A truck moving in +y slows from 10 m/s to 6 m/s in 10 s. What is its acceleration? Speeding up or slowing down?

a = (6 − 10) ÷ 10 = −0.40 m/s². Velocity is + and acceleration is −. Opposite signs, so it is slowing down.

Can an object have zero velocity and a nonzero acceleration at the same instant?

Yes. At the bottom of a squat or the top of a jump, the COM’s velocity is 0 for an instant, but its acceleration is not 0, because the velocity is changing direction.

In-class: partner challenge

  1. Predict: was Bolt running at 10.44 m/s for the whole race? Then test: drag the secant to 0 and 9.58 s (slope 10.44 m/s), then scrub the time cursor and compare the tangent.
  2. Predict: which 20 m segment was fastest? Then test: turn on Segment averages.
  3. Predict: in Build your own, with v₀ = +8 m/s and a = −2 m/s², when does the velocity reach 0, and what happens after that? Then test: press Slowing down, still moving forward and scrub.

Discuss

  • Is 10.44 m/s Bolt’s speed, his velocity, or both?
  • A runner loses a 100 m race. Did they accelerate less out of the blocks, or slow down more at the end? How would the graphs show each?

θ, ω, and α: an elbow curl

The Kinematics Graph Linker, opened on the elbow curl. Keep the units in radians. Open it in its own window.

Pre-class: guided steps

  1. Switch Angle units to Degrees, then back to Radians. The curl goes from −30° to +30°, which is −0.52 rad to +0.52 rad. Stay in radians from here on.
  2. Press Play. Where is the θ curve steepest? That is where ω is largest.
  3. Scrub to 1.50 s. ω is at its peak, and α crosses 0.
  4. Turn on Average (secant). With t₁ = 0 and t₂ = 3 s, its slope is the average ω, π/9 ≈ 0.35 rad/s. The peak ω is higher, about 0.65 rad/s.

Check yourself

Convert 72° to radians, and 1.4 rad to degrees.

72 × (π ÷ 180) = 1.26 rad. 1.4 × (180 ÷ π) = 80.21°.

A right knee goes from −0.17 to −1.75 rad in 1.5 s (squat) and from −0.26 to −1.31 rad in 0.20 s (landing). Find Δθ and ω for each.

Squat: Δθ = −1.75 − (−0.17) = −1.58 rad, ω = −1.58 ÷ 1.5 = −1.05 rad/s. Landing: Δθ = −1.31 − (−0.26) = −1.05 rad, ω = −1.05 ÷ 0.20 = −5.25 rad/s. Both are negative because knee flexion is − in our system.

A segment starts at rest and reaches 50 rad/s clockwise in 5 s. What is its angular acceleration?

Clockwise is −, so Δω = −50 rad/s and α = −50 ÷ 5 = −10.00 rad/s².

In-class: partner challenge

  1. Predict: where in the curl is ω largest, and what is α at that moment? Then test: scrub with Tangent lines on.
  2. Predict: give the signs of ω and α at 0.5 s and at 2.5 s. Speeding up or slowing down? Then test: scrub to each time and read the “Read the graph” box.
  3. Predict: the hand is 0.40 m from the elbow. How fast does it move at the peak ω? Then test: read the peak ω and use v = ω·r.

Discuss

  • Why is the average ω (0.35 rad/s) lower than the peak ω?
  • Why does v = ω·r only work with ω in rad/s?

A squat on a force plate

vGRF = m·(g + a). Up is +. Scrub through the squat and watch the COM’s velocity and acceleration decide whether vGRF is above or below body weight.

Pre-class: guided steps

Lecture 4: Newton’s laws

  1. Set Time to 0.00 s (standing still) and Body mass to 60 kg. vGRF equals the weight, W = m·g = 588.60 N, or 1.00 BW.
  2. Change the mass while standing still. vGRF always equals m·g, because a = 0 (first law).

Lecture 5: Center of mass and GRF

  1. Set the mass to 70 kg (keep depth 0.40 m and squat time 2.0 s) and press Play. The Phase readout names each part of the squat. Note the sign of COM velocity and COM acceleration in each.
  2. Drag Time to 1.50 s (the bottom). Velocity is about 0, but acceleration is + and vGRF is above 1 BW.

Check yourself

Lecture 4: what net force accelerates an 80 kg person at 1.5 m/s²?

F_net = m·a = 80 × 1.5 = 120.00 N, in the direction of the acceleration.

Lecture 4: are a resting book’s weight and the table pushing up on it a third-law pair?

No. Both act on the book. They are equal because the book isn’t accelerating (first law). The table’s push pairs with the book pushing down on the table.

Lecture 5: a 60 kg person accelerates upward at 1.5 m/s². What does the force plate read?

vGRF = m·(g + a) = 60 × (9.81 + 1.5) = 678.60 N, which is 1.15 BW.

Lecture 5: give the sign of COM velocity and acceleration in each squat phase.

Early descent: v −, a −. Late descent: v −, a +. Ascent push: v +, a +. Near the top: v +, a −.

In-class: partner challenge

Lecture 4: Newton’s laws

  1. Predict: a 70 kg person stands still on the plate. What does it read? Then test: set 70 kg and Time 0.00 s. What is the third-law partner of this vGRF, and what does it act on?

Lecture 5: Center of mass and GRF

  1. Predict: during a slow squat, when do you feel lighter, and when heavier? Then test: press Play and watch when vGRF drops below and rises above 1 BW.
  2. Predict: vGRF for an 80 kg lifter at the bottom of the squat. Then test: set 80 kg, drag Time to 1.50 s, read the COM acceleration, work out m·(g + a) by hand, and compare with the readout.
  3. Predict: does a faster squat raise or lower the vGRF peak? Then test: change squat time from 2.0 s to 1.0 s.

Discuss

  • What does the plate read while a jumper is in the air? Why can vGRF never be negative?
  • Walking vGRF peaks just above 1 BW, running between 2 and 3 BW. Why does running need a bigger upward COM acceleration?

Levers and net torque

The fulcrum sits at 0. The effort pushes up and the load pulls down. τ = force × perpendicular distance; counterclockwise (as drawn) is +.

Pre-class: guided steps

  1. Press Elbow curl (class III). Read the lever class, the TMA (0.17) and the net torque (−5.00 N·m). The load side wins.
  2. Set the effort position to 0.05 m and the load position to 0.35 m. TMA = 0.05 ÷ 0.35 = 0.14, and the effort is in the middle, so it is class III.
  3. Press Practice problem 4 (271 N load at 0.34 m, effort at 0.13 m). Read the effort needed to balance. Then raise the effort until the net torque turns +.
  4. Press Calf raise (class II). The load sits between the fulcrum and the effort, so the TMA is above 1.

Check yourself

A muscle pulls with 400 N at a moment arm of 0.04 m. How much torque does it make?

τ = F × d⊥ = 400 × 0.04 = 16.00 N·m.

Effort arm 0.05 m, load arm 0.35 m, effort in the middle. TMA and class? Could a class II lever have this TMA?

TMA = 0.05 ÷ 0.35 = 0.14. The effort is in the middle, so it is class III. No: a class II lever’s effort arm is always longer than its load arm, so its TMA is always greater than 1.

A 271 N load acts 0.34 m from the fulcrum and the effort acts 0.13 m from it. What is the smallest effort that moves the load?

Load torque = 271 × 0.34 = 92.14 N·m. The effort torque has to be larger: E × 0.13 > 92.14, so E > 708.77 N.

In-class: partner challenge

  1. Predict: a 100 N effort at 0.45 m and a 100 N load at 0.15 m. Which side wins, and what is the TMA? What if you swap the two positions? Then test: set the four sliders, then swap.
  2. Predict: the smallest effort for Practice problem 4. Then test: press it and raise the effort until the net torque turns +.
  3. Predict: a 30 N dumbbell is held 0.35 m from the elbow. The biceps inserts 0.04 m from the elbow and pulls perpendicular to the forearm. What biceps force holds it still? Then test: set the effort at 0.04 m and a 30 N load at 0.35 m, then read the balancing effort.

Discuss

  • Does the TMA alone tell you which side wins? What do you compare instead?
  • If class III levers need so much muscle force, why does the body use them so often?

Angle of pull: measure from the segment

Simplified biceps: origin 0.30 m up the humerus, insertion 0.05 m along the forearm. Change the elbow angle and watch θ change. Raise the whole arm and watch θ stay put while the dumbbell’s moment arm changes.

Pre-class: guided steps

  1. Set Elbow flexion to 0.52, 1.75 and 2.44 rad. Read θ at each (the tool shows 0.45, 1.58 and 2.32 rad). θ grows as the elbow flexes.
  2. At each angle, read the parallel component. Does it point toward the elbow (stabilizing), or away from it (dislocating)? Near 1.75 rad it is close to 0.
  3. Set the elbow to 1.57 rad and the dumbbell to 50 N, and read the force needed to hold still. Then raise Shoulder flexion to 1.05 rad. θ stays put, but the dumbbell’s moment arm drops from 0.30 m to about 0.15 m.

Check yourself

A biceps pulls with 800 N at θ = 0.70 rad and inserts 0.04 m from the elbow. Rotary component, parallel component and torque?

Rotary = 800 × sin 0.70 = 515.37 N. Parallel = 800 × cos 0.70 = 611.87 N, toward the elbow (θ < 1.57 rad). Torque = 515.37 × 0.04 = 20.61 N·m.

A 40 N weight is held 0.35 m from the elbow, elbow at 1.57 rad. Its torque with the forearm horizontal, and 0.52 rad above horizontal? Did θ change?

Horizontal: 40 × 0.35 × cos 0 = 14.00 N·m. 0.52 rad above: 40 × 0.35 × cos 0.52 = 12.15 N·m. Both tend to extend the elbow, so they are −14.00 and −12.15 N·m in our system. θ did not change; it depends only on the elbow angle.

Is the parallel component stabilizing or dislocating at θ = 0.45 rad? At θ = 2.32 rad?

cos 0.45 = 0.90, which is positive, so it pulls toward the elbow: stabilizing. cos 2.32 = −0.68, which is negative, so it pulls away: dislocating.

In-class: partner challenge

  1. Predict: at what elbow angle does θ reach 1.57 rad, and what happens to the parallel component there? Then test: drag the elbow until θ reads 1.57 rad.
  2. Predict: a 500 N biceps force with the elbow at 1.05 rad (θ ≈ 0.91 rad): rotary, parallel (toward or away?) and torque. Then test: set 1.05 rad and 500 N. The tool uses the unrounded θ (it shows 0.92 rad), so its answers differ slightly. Now raise the shoulder. Does θ change?
  3. Predict: with the elbow at 1.57 rad and a 50 N dumbbell, which needs more biceps force: the upper arm at your side, or the shoulder flexed 1.05 rad? Then test: read Force needed to hold still at both shoulder angles.

Discuss

  • In a preacher curl the upper arm is tilted forward on a pad. Is the hardest point at a larger or smaller elbow angle than in a standing curl?
  • Name one reason strength changes through the range of motion.
